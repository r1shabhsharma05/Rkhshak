# Content and E-E-A-T

## Google's Who / How / Why test
| Question | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **Who** created it? | Visible byline, author bio page, professional credentials. Non-negotiable where readers expect it, and for YMYL topics. |
| **How** was it created? | Process disclosure where readers would reasonably ask, especially for AI-assisted content. Original research, first-hand evidence, lived experience. |
| **Why** does it exist? | "To help people" rather than "to attract search clicks." Watch for niche entry without expertise, churn for freshness signals, and writing to a word-count target. |

## E-E-A-T framework
- **Experience**: first-hand signals — the author did it, used it, was there. Photos, specifics, dates, mistakes.
- **Expertise**: the author knows the topic deeply; credentials where the topic demands them.
- **Authoritativeness**: others cite, link to, and reference this source; recognised in the field.
- **Trustworthiness**: accurate, honest, transparent about who runs the site, how to contact them, and how content is made. Trust is the load-bearing letter — the other three do not compensate for its absence.

## Content metrics
| Page type | Minimum words |
|---|---|
| Homepage | 500 |
| Service page | 800 |
| Blog post | 1,500 |
| Product page | 300+ (400+ for complex products) |
| Location page | 500–600 |

Word count is a floor, not a target — a page that answers the query in 400 words beats a 2,000-word page that does not. Also check: readability (short paragraphs, subheadings, bullets), keyword use that reads naturally, content structure (one idea per section), multimedia that adds information, internal links with descriptive anchors, external links to authoritative sources.

## AI content assessment
Acceptable: AI-assisted content that is accurate, original, useful, and disclosed where readers would ask. Low-quality markers: generic claims with no specifics, no first-hand evidence, factual errors, repetitive phrasing, content written to a keyword rather than a question. Judge the page, not the tool — the test is whether the page helps, not how it was drafted.

## Draft cleanup
- Strip invisible characters (zero-width spaces and joiners, soft hyphens, byte-order marks, tag characters, invisible separators, non-breaking and narrow spaces). A keyboard does not produce these; they survive copy-paste and are invisible in every editor.
- Replace AI-typical phrasing with plain words — but only with 1:1 swaps that keep the sentence grammatical. Never rewrite meaning to remove a phrase.
- Hidden text (same-colour text, off-screen text, tiny text) is a violation, not an optimisation. Flag and remove.

## AI citation readiness (GEO signals)
- Structured answers: clear question-answer formats, definition patterns, step-by-step instructions an answer engine can extract and cite.
- First-party data: original research, statistics, case studies, unique datasets — the most-cited material.
- Schema markup: Article and other relevant types; FAQPage no longer produces Google FAQ rich results — use QAPage only for genuine user Q&A.
- Topical authority: clusters of deep content, not isolated pages.
- Entity clarity: brand, authors, and key concepts defined clearly, backed by Organization and Person schema.
- Track visibility across Google AI Overviews, AI Mode, ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Bing Copilot — not just traditional rankings. Treat AI citation as its own KPI.

## Freshness
Show publication or update dates. Refresh pages whose facts changed; do not fake freshness by changing a date on unchanged content. For time-sensitive topics, recency matters; for evergreen topics, accuracy matters more than the date.

## Google update correlation
When traffic drops, check the date against known core and spam updates before blaming the page. A drop that starts on an update date is an update story first and a page story second — and a thin-content or scaled-content pattern is the usual suspect, not a single keyword.
